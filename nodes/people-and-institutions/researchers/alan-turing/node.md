Alan Turing (1912 to 1954) was a British mathematician and logician, born in London. {#c-years}
His 1936 paper "On Computable Numbers" gave a definition of computation and a limit on what computation can achieve. {#c-1936-founding}
His 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" replaced the question "Can machines think?" with the imitation game.
Andrew Hodges writes that Turing's contention, that a properly programmed computer could rival the brain, founded the artificial intelligence program of the following decades. {#c-founded-ai}

## Contributions

The edges to the items he made are generated; the note below gives his main works in his own terms.

Turing (1936) describes the computable numbers as the real numbers whose expressions as a decimal are calculable by finite means. {#c-computable}
The paper describes a universal machine U, which, given the description of a machine M, computes the same sequence as M. {#c-universal}
In Section 11 he applies the results to show that the Hilbert Entscheidungsproblem can have no solution. {#c-entscheidungsproblem}

Turing (1950) proposes to replace the question "Can machines think?" by a game, the imitation game, played by a man, a woman and an interrogator. {#c-imitation-game}
The new question asks what will happen when a machine takes the part of the man, and whether the interrogator will then decide wrongly as often. {#c-machine-takes-part}
Turing predicts that in about fifty years an average interrogator will have at most a 70 per cent chance of the right identification after five minutes. {#c-forecast}
The paper then answers objections to this view, beginning with the theological objection and the mathematical objection. {#c-objections}
One of them is Lady Lovelace's objection, that the Analytical Engine has no pretensions to originate anything. {#c-lovelace}
In the last section he proposes to simulate the mind of a child instead of the mind of an adult, and then to educate it. {#c-child-machine}

In 1948 Turing suggested systems that modify their own programs, among them nets of logical components, "unorganised machines", that could be trained into a desired function. {#c-unorganised}
Hodges writes that Turing, with the expression "genetical or evolutionary search", anticipated genetic algorithms. {#c-genetic}

## Career

Turing studied mathematics at King's College, Cambridge, and was elected a Fellow of the college in 1935. {#c-kings}
He did further work in logic at Princeton and returned to Britain in 1938. {#c-princeton}
From 1939 to 1945 he worked at Bletchley Park on the German cipher machine Enigma, with a particular responsibility for reading the U-boat communications. {#c-bletchley}
At the end of the war the National Physical Laboratory commissioned his plans for an electronic computer. {#c-npl}
In 1948 he moved to Manchester University to plan software for its computer, and he wrote the 1950 paper there. {#c-manchester}
At Manchester he held the post of Reader in the Theory of Computing. {#c-reader}
From 1950 he worked on a mathematical theory of morphogenesis, based on non-linear equations for chemical reaction and diffusion. {#c-morphogenesis}
