A diffusion model is a generative model that learns to reverse a fixed noising process.
The forward process adds a little Gaussian noise to a data point at each of $T$ steps until nothing of the data is left, and a neural network is trained to take a noisy point and the step number and predict the noise that was added.
To generate, the model starts from pure noise and removes the predicted noise step by step.
Sohl-Dickstein and colleagues describe the essential idea, inspired by non-equilibrium statistical physics, as systematically and slowly destroying structure in a data distribution through an iterative forward diffusion process, then learning a reverse diffusion process that restores structure in data, which yields a flexible and tractable generative model with thousands of layers or time steps. {#c-sd-def}
Diffusion models are the standard generators of images, video and audio, and the base of text-to-image systems.

## In plain words

Take a photograph and add a faint layer of static to it, then another, and another, a thousand times.
After a few hundred layers the picture is hard to make out, and at the end it is pure static, the same kind of static whatever the photograph was.
Undoing all thousand layers at once is hopeless, but undoing one is not: a slightly noisy photograph looks like a photograph, and a network can learn to guess which speckles are noise.
A diffusion model is such a network, trained on millions of photographs noised to every level, and asked one question: what noise was added here?
To make a new image, it starts from fresh static, guesses the noise, removes a little of it, and repeats, a thousand times or, with faster samplers, a few dozen.
Each small step only has to make the picture a bit less noisy, so the network never has to produce an image in one shot, and the image that comes out is new, because the static it started from was.

## Overview

The forward process is a [[m2:multivariate-normal-distribution|Gaussian]] Markov chain on $\mathbf{x}_0 \in \mathbb{R}^D$, the data point, with variances $\beta_1, \dots, \beta_T$ in $(0, 1)$:

$$ q(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_{t-1}) = \mathcal{N}\big(\mathbf{x}_t; \sqrt{1 - \beta_t}\, \mathbf{x}_{t-1}, \beta_t I\big) . $$ {#eq-forward-step}

The factor $\sqrt{1 - \beta_t}$ shrinks the point as the noise is added, so that the variance stays bounded.
With $\alpha_t = 1 - \beta_t$ and $\bar\alpha_t = \prod_{s=1}^{t} \alpha_s$, the steps compose in closed form,

$$ q(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_0) = \mathcal{N}\big(\mathbf{x}_t; \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, \mathbf{x}_0, (1 - \bar\alpha_t) I\big), \qquad \mathbf{x}_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, \mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, \boldsymbol\epsilon, \quad \boldsymbol\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I), $$ {#eq-forward-marginal}

so $\mathbf{x}_t$ at any step is drawn in one shot. {#c-forward-marginal}
The derivation `forward-marginal` proves it.
The schedule is chosen so that $\bar\alpha_T \approx 0$, and $\mathbf{x}_T$ is then nearly a standard normal whatever $\mathbf{x}_0$ was.

The generative model runs the chain backwards, from $p(\mathbf{x}_T) = \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ through learned Gaussian steps $p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t) = \mathcal{N}\big(\boldsymbol\mu_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t), \sigma_t^2 I\big)$.
Gaussian steps are the right family when the forward steps are small: the paper grounds the construction in the Kolmogorov forward and backward equations, according to which, for many forward diffusion processes the reverse diffusion process can be described using the same functional form, so that a forward process of small Gaussian steps has a reverse process of Gaussian steps whose means and variances are learned. {#c-sd-same-form}
Conditioned on the data point, the reverse step is exactly Gaussian by [[m2:bayes-theorem|Bayes' rule]],

$$ q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{x}_0) = \mathcal{N}\big(\tilde{\boldsymbol\mu}_t, \tilde\beta_t I\big), \quad \tilde{\boldsymbol\mu}_t = \frac{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\, \beta_t}{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, \mathbf{x}_0 + \frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\, (1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1})}{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, \mathbf{x}_t, \quad \tilde\beta_t = \frac{1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1}}{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, \beta_t , $$ {#eq-posterior}

with $\bar\alpha_0 = 1$; so every term of the variational bound is a [[m2:kullback-leibler-divergence|KL divergence]] between Gaussians. {#c-posterior}
The derivation `posterior` proves it.

Ho, Jain and Abbeel present diffusion probabilistic models as a class of latent variable models inspired by considerations from nonequilibrium thermodynamics, and obtain their best results by training on a weighted variational bound designed according to a connection between diffusion probabilistic models and denoising score matching with Langevin dynamics. {#c-ddpm-def}
The model predicts the noise instead of the mean.
Writing

$$ \boldsymbol\mu_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}} \Big( \mathbf{x}_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}}\, \boldsymbol\epsilon_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t) \Big), $$ {#eq-reverse-mean}

the reverse mean equals the posterior mean $\tilde{\boldsymbol\mu}_t$ exactly when $\boldsymbol\epsilon_\theta$ returns the noise $\boldsymbol\epsilon$ that produced $\mathbf{x}_t$ from $\mathbf{x}_0$. {#c-reverse-mean}
Then each KL term of the bound is a weighted squared error of the noise prediction, eq-eps-weight below, and DDPM trains on the unweighted version, the simple loss

$$ L_\text{simple}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{t, \mathbf{x}_0, \boldsymbol\epsilon} \Big[ \big\lVert \boldsymbol\epsilon - \boldsymbol\epsilon_\theta\big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, \mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, \boldsymbol\epsilon, t\big) \big\rVert^2 \Big], \qquad t \sim \mathcal{U}\{1, \dots, T\} . $$ {#eq-simple-loss}

Training is regression: draw a data point, a step and a noise, noise the point by eq-forward-marginal, and regress the network's output on the noise with the [[m2:mean-squared-error|mean squared error]].
There is no adversary, no encoder and no sampling during training.
Sampling runs the reverse chain, eq-ddpm-step below, from $\mathbf{x}_T \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$.

The noise predictor is also a score estimator.
The minimizer over all functions of the noise-prediction loss at step $t$ is the [[m2:conditional-expectation|conditional mean]] $\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol\epsilon \mid \mathbf{x}_t]$, and

$$ \boldsymbol\epsilon^*(\mathbf{x}_t, t) = \mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol\epsilon \mid \mathbf{x}_t] = -\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\; \nabla_{\mathbf{x}_t} \log q_t(\mathbf{x}_t), $$ {#eq-tweedie}

where $q_t$ is the density of the data noised to step $t$, so a trained noise predictor is a scaled estimate of the score. {#c-tweedie}
The derivation `tweedie-score` proves it.
This is the bridge to score-based models, which learn $\nabla \log q_t$ directly and sample with Langevin dynamics, and to the stochastic-differential-equation view, in which DDPM is one discretization of a continuous process.

## Components

The notation has vectors as rows; a layer maps $\mathbf{h}$ to $\mathbf{h} W + \mathbf{b}$ with $W$ of shape $(n_\text{in}, n_\text{out})$.

**The schedule.** The variances $\beta_t$ rise from $\beta_1$ to $\beta_T$; DDPM uses a linear schedule.
The DDPM experiments set $T = 1000$ and a forward variance increasing linearly from $\beta_1 = 10^{-4}$ to $\beta_T = 0.02$, chosen to be small relative to data scaled to $[-1, 1]$ and so that the last step's term $L_T$ is close to zero. {#c-ddpm-setup}
With these constants $\bar\alpha_T \approx 4 \times 10^{-5}$, so $\mathbf{x}_T$ keeps less than one percent of the data's scale, and $L_T$ is about $10^{-5}$ bits per dimension; `test_schedule_of_ho_et_al` checks both.
Later work replaced the linear schedule by a cosine one and by schedules in continuous time; the code of this page keeps the linear one with the small $T = 10$ of the shape contract and $\beta$ from 0.02 to 0.5, so that the tests run in seconds.

**The time embedding.** The network gets the step as a vector $\mathbf{e}(t) \in \mathbb{R}^E$ of sines and cosines at geometric frequencies,

$$ e_{2i}(t) = \sin(t\, \omega_i), \quad e_{2i+1}(t) = \cos(t\, \omega_i), \qquad \omega_i = 10000^{-2i/E} . $$ {#eq-time-embedding}

To represent the reverse process DDPM uses a U-Net backbone similar to an unmasked PixelCNN++ with group normalization throughout; the parameters are shared across time, which is given to the network with the Transformer sinusoidal position embedding, and self-attention is used at the $16 \times 16$ feature map resolution. {#c-ddpm-arch}
One network serves every step, so it has to know which step it is at: the noise level fixes how much of the input is signal.

**The noise predictor.** The network of this page is a [[multilayer-perceptron]] with one tanh hidden layer of $H$ units on the concatenation of $\mathbf{x}_t$ and $\mathbf{e}(t)$:

$$ \boldsymbol\epsilon_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t) = \tanh\big([\mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{e}(t)]\, W_1 + \mathbf{b}_1\big)\, W_2 + \mathbf{b}_2 . $$ {#eq-eps-model}

$W_2$ and $\mathbf{b}_2$ start at 0, so the untrained network predicts no noise.
For images the predictor is a U-Net or a [[transformer]] on patches, as in the [[vision-transformer]], with [[residual-connection|residual blocks]] and [[group-normalization]]; the diffusion machinery around it does not change.

**The ancestral step.** One step of the reverse chain, Algorithm 2 of DDPM, is

$$ \mathbf{x}_{t-1} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}} \Big( \mathbf{x}_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}}\, \boldsymbol\epsilon_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t) \Big) + \sigma_t \mathbf{z}, \qquad \mathbf{z} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I), $$ {#eq-ddpm-step}

with $\mathbf{z} = 0$ at the last step.
Ho, Jain and Abbeel set the reverse process variances to untrained time dependent constants; experimentally, $\sigma_t^2 = \beta_t$ and $\sigma_t^2 = \tilde\beta_t$ had similar results, the first choice being optimal for $\mathbf{x}_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ and the second for $\mathbf{x}_0$ deterministically set to one point. {#c-ddpm-sigma}

**The DDIM step.** Song, Meng and Ermon generalize DDPMs through a class of non-Markovian diffusion processes that lead to the same training objective; their generative processes can be deterministic, which gives implicit models that, by the authors' measurements, produce high quality samples 10 to 50 times faster in wall-clock time than DDPMs. {#c-ddim-def}
From step $t$ to any earlier step $s$, with the predicted data point $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0 = (\mathbf{x}_t - \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, \boldsymbol\epsilon_\theta) / \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$,

$$ \mathbf{x}_s = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_s}\, \hat{\mathbf{x}}_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_s - \sigma^2}\; \boldsymbol\epsilon_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t) + \sigma \mathbf{z}, $$ {#eq-ddim-step}

$$ \sigma = \eta \sqrt{\frac{1 - \bar\alpha_s}{1 - \bar\alpha_t} \Big(1 - \frac{\bar\alpha_t}{\bar\alpha_s}\Big)} . $$ {#eq-ddim-sigma}

For $s < t$ and any $\sigma$ with $0 \le \sigma^2 \le 1 - \bar\alpha_s$, the step with the true noise has the forward marginal $\mathcal{N}\big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_s}\, \mathbf{x}_0, (1 - \bar\alpha_s) I\big)$, so a noise predictor trained once serves every $\sigma$ and every sub-sequence of steps. {#c-ddim-marginals}
With $s = t - 1$ and $\eta = 1$, $\sigma^2 = \tilde\beta_t$ and the DDIM step is the posterior step of DDPM with $\sigma_t^2 = \tilde\beta_t$; with $\eta = 0$ it is a deterministic map from $\mathbf{x}_t$ to $\mathbf{x}_s$. {#c-ddim-eta-one}
The derivation `ddim-marginals` proves both.
The deterministic sampler skips steps: it runs eq-ddim-step along a sub-sequence $\tau_1 < \dots < \tau_S = T$ of $S$ steps, a few dozen instead of a thousand.

**Guidance.** A conditional model $\boldsymbol\epsilon_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, c)$, given a class or a text $c$, can be pushed towards samples that match the condition more strongly than the data do.
Dhariwal and Nichol guide a diffusion model with a classifier $p_\phi(y \mid \mathbf{x}_t)$ of noisy images and a gradient scale $s$: classifier guided diffusion sampling shifts the mean of each Gaussian transition to $\boldsymbol\mu + s \Sigma \nabla \log p_\phi(y \mid \mathbf{x}_t)$, and classifier guided DDIM sampling uses the score of the joint distribution, the new epsilon prediction $\boldsymbol\epsilon_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t) - \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, \nabla \log p_\phi(y \mid \mathbf{x}_t)$. {#c-dn-guidance}
Ho and Salimans find that guidance can be performed by a pure generative model without a classifier: in classifier-free guidance a conditional and an unconditional diffusion model are trained jointly and their score estimates are combined to trade off sample quality and diversity, as classifier guidance does. {#c-cfg-def}
Classifier-free guidance uses a single network for both models, giving the unconditional model a null token $\varnothing$ for the class identifier, and trains it by randomly replacing the condition with the null token with a probability $p_\text{uncond}$ set as a hyperparameter. {#c-cfg-null}
The sampler then uses, with a guidance strength $w \ge 0$,

$$ \tilde{\boldsymbol\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, c) = (1 + w)\, \boldsymbol\epsilon_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, c) - w\, \boldsymbol\epsilon_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, \varnothing) . $$ {#eq-guidance}

With exact noise predictions, the guided prediction equals $-\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}$ times the gradient of $\log q_t(\mathbf{x}_t \mid c) + w \log q_t(c \mid \mathbf{x}_t)$, so it is the noise prediction of the conditional density tilted by the $w$-th power of an implicit classifier that is never trained. {#c-guidance-implicit}
The derivation `classifier-free-guidance` proves it from eq-tweedie and Bayes' rule.
Ho and Salimans note that the guided prediction contains no classifier gradient and that there may in general be no classifier for which it is the classifier-guided score; it is inspired by the gradient of an implicit classifier proportional to $p(\mathbf{z} \mid c) / p(\mathbf{z})$. {#c-cfg-not-classifier}

## Computation and cost

One evaluation of the noise predictor on $B$ points costs

$$ C = 2BH(2D + E) $$ {#eq-flops}

FLOPs in its two products, a multiply-add counted as two.
The noise predictor of the shape contract, one tanh hidden layer of $H$ units on the $D$ data coordinates and an $E$-dimensional time embedding, has $(D + E + 1) H + (H + 1) D$ parameters and costs $2BH(2D + E)$ FLOPs on $B$ points; an ancestral sample costs $T$ evaluations and a DDIM sample one per step of its sub-sequence. {#c-params}
In the contract,

{shape:flops_forward}

which is {shape:flops_forward@example} FLOPs for the example and {shape:flops_forward@two_modes} for `two_modes` at its batch of 256.
A training step evaluates the network once per point, at one random step, and the backward pass costs about twice that.
Sampling is the expensive part: a sample costs $T$ evaluations, run one after the other.
The DDIM paper reports that it takes around 20 hours to sample 50k images of size $32 \times 32$ from a DDPM, against less than a minute from a GAN, on an Nvidia 2080 Ti GPU, and nearly 1000 hours for 50k images of size $256 \times 256$, because all $T$ steps have to be performed sequentially. {#c-ddim-slow}
Faster samplers cut the number of evaluations: DDIM to a few dozen, higher-order ODE solvers to 10 or 20, and distillation to one to four.
Classifier-free guidance doubles the cost of each step, which evaluates the conditional and the unconditional prediction, unless the two are batched together.
Latent diffusion moves the process into the code space of an [[autoencoder]], which shrinks $D$ by one to two orders of magnitude for images.

## Training

Algorithm 1 of DDPM repeats: draw $\mathbf{x}_0$ from the data, $t$ uniformly from $\{1, \dots, T\}$ and $\boldsymbol\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$, and take a gradient step on $\lVert \boldsymbol\epsilon - \boldsymbol\epsilon_\theta(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, \mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, \boldsymbol\epsilon, t) \rVert^2$.
The code uses [[adam]] and [[backpropagation]] through the network; the NumPy reference writes the backward pass by hand.

### The variational bound and its weights

As in a [[variational-autoencoder]] whose encoder is fixed, the negative log-likelihood is bounded by the [[al:evidence-lower-bound|evidence lower bound]]:

$$ -\log p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_0) \le \mathbb{E}_q\Big[ \underbrace{D_\text{KL}\big(q(\mathbf{x}_T \mid \mathbf{x}_0) \,\Vert\, p(\mathbf{x}_T)\big)}_{L_T} + \sum_{t = 2}^{T} \underbrace{D_\text{KL}\big(q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{x}_0) \,\Vert\, p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t)\big)}_{L_{t-1}} \underbrace{- \log p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_0 \mid \mathbf{x}_1)}_{L_0} \Big] . $$ {#eq-elbo}

$L_T$ has no parameters, and with eq-posterior and eq-reverse-mean each middle term is a weighted noise error,

$$ L_{t-1} = \frac{\beta_t^2}{2 \sigma_t^2\, \alpha_t\, (1 - \bar\alpha_t)}\; \mathbb{E}\big\lVert \boldsymbol\epsilon - \boldsymbol\epsilon_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t) \big\rVert^2 + \text{const} . $$ {#eq-eps-weight}

With the reverse mean in the noise parameterization and a fixed variance $\sigma_t^2$, the KL term of the bound is this weighted squared error, so the bound is a weighted noise-prediction loss and the simple loss drops the weight. {#c-eps-weight}
The derivation `eps-parameterization` proves it.
For the schedule of DDPM with $\sigma_t^2 = \beta_t$ the weight is $\beta_t / (2\alpha_t(1 - \bar\alpha_t))$, which falls from 0.5 at $t = 1$ to about 0.01 at $t = T$, so eq-simple-loss gives the noisy steps far more weight than the bound does, and the network spends its capacity on the hard denoising tasks that shape the overall structure of the samples.
Ho and colleagues write that the eps-prediction parameterization both resembles Langevin dynamics and simplifies the diffusion model's variational bound to an objective that resembles denoising score matching, and that it is nonetheless just another parameterization of the reverse step, whose effectiveness they test in an ablation; predicting $\mathbf{x}_0$ led to worse sample quality early in their experiments. {#c-ddpm-dsm}
In the ablation of Ho and colleagues, predicting the posterior mean works well only when trained on the true variational bound, learning the reverse variances leads to unstable training and poorer samples than fixed variances, and predicting the noise does about as well as predicting the mean on the bound but much better with the simplified objective; training on the true bound yields better codelengths, and the simplified objective the best sample quality. {#c-ddpm-ablation}

### What the loss can reach

The loss does not go to zero.
The best possible predictor is $\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol\epsilon \mid \mathbf{x}_t]$, and the noise is not a function of $\mathbf{x}_t$: at large $t$, where $\mathbf{x}_t$ is mostly noise, the predictor can recover most of $\boldsymbol\epsilon$, but at small $t$ many noises are consistent with the same $\mathbf{x}_t$ because the data point is unknown.
The floor is the average conditional variance of $\boldsymbol\epsilon$, and for data that are a mixture of Gaussians the NumPy reference computes it exactly with `mixture_eps`; on the two modes of the test it is about 0.50 per point.

## Inductive biases

A diffusion model assumes that data can be produced by reversing a slow corruption by Gaussian noise, and it splits generation into many small denoising problems, each easy, all solved by one network told the noise level.
At high noise the problems are about global layout and at low noise about texture; the schedule and the weighting decide how much capacity each gets.
Because the reverse steps are modelled as Gaussian, the model is accurate only when the steps are small, so fewer steps trade quality for speed unless the sampler is changed.
The network's own biases matter as much as in any model: a U-Net's convolutions give translation equivariance and multi-scale features, and transformers on patches scale with data and compute.
Unlike a [[normalizing-flow]], the model needs no invertible architecture, and unlike a [[generative-adversarial-network]] it has no adversary: training is regression with a fixed target, which is stable and covers the modes of the data.

## Evidence

Song and Ermon produce samples with Langevin dynamics using gradients of the data distribution estimated with score matching; because the gradients can be ill-defined and hard to estimate when the data reside on low-dimensional manifolds, they perturb the data with different levels of Gaussian noise, jointly estimate the scores for all noise levels, and sample with annealed Langevin dynamics that uses gradients for decreasing noise levels. {#c-ncsn-def}
Song and Ermon argue that because the support of Gaussian noise is the whole space, the perturbed data are not confined to a low-dimensional manifold and score estimation becomes well defined, and that large Gaussian noise fills low-density regions of the unperturbed distribution, which helps both score estimation and the mixing of Langevin dynamics between modes. {#c-ncsn-noise}
The noise conditional score network sampled with annealed Langevin dynamics reached an Inception score of 8.87 and an FID of 25.32 on CIFAR-10. {#c-ncsn-results}

The unconditional DDPM trained on the simplified objective reached an Inception score of 9.46 and an FID of 3.17 on CIFAR-10, with a negative log likelihood of at most 3.75 bits per dimension on the test set; the model trained on the true bound reached at most 3.70. On $256 \times 256$ LSUN the FID was 7.89 for Church and 4.90 for Bedroom. {#c-ddpm-results}
The gap between train and test codelengths was at most 0.03 bits per dimension, and the lossless codelengths were not competitive with other likelihood-based models; treating $L_1 + \dots + L_T$ as rate and $L_0$ as distortion, the CIFAR-10 model with the best samples had a rate of 1.78 and a distortion of 1.97 bits per dimension, and more than half of the lossless codelength describes imperceptible distortions. {#c-ddpm-lossy}

Song and colleagues describe a stochastic differential equation that transforms a data distribution to a known prior by slowly injecting noise, and a reverse-time SDE that depends only on the time-dependent score of the perturbed data distribution; they derive an equivalent neural ODE, the probability flow, that samples from the same distribution and enables exact likelihood computation. {#c-sde-def}
In the SDE view the noise perturbations of score matching with Langevin dynamics and of DDPM are discretizations of two SDEs: the first always gives a process whose variance explodes, the variance exploding SDE, and the second a process of fixed unit variance when the initial distribution has unit variance, the variance preserving SDE; DDPM's ancestral sampling is one discretization of the reverse-time VP SDE. {#c-sde-vp}
With architectural improvements the score SDE models reached an Inception score of 9.89 and an FID of 2.20 for unconditional CIFAR-10 generation and a likelihood of 2.99 bits per dimension, and generated $1024 \times 1024$ images. {#c-sde-results}

With the deterministic DDIM sampler, images generated from the same $\mathbf{x}_T$ along different sub-sequences of steps share most high-level features, and samples with only 20 steps are already very similar in high-level features to ones with 1000 steps, which the authors read as $\mathbf{x}_T$ being an informative latent encoding of the image. {#c-ddim-latent}

With an improved architecture and classifier guidance, diffusion models reached an FID of 2.97 on ImageNet $128 \times 128$, 4.59 on $256 \times 256$ and 7.72 on $512 \times 512$, matched BigGAN-deep with as few as 25 forward passes per sample, and with upsampling diffusion models reached 3.94 on $256 \times 256$ and 3.85 on $512 \times 512$. {#c-dn-results}
Conditioning an unconditional model on the class Pembroke Welsh corgi, a classifier scale of 1.0 gave an FID of 33.0 and samples that were not convincingly of the class, while a scale of 10.0 gave an FID of 12.0 and much more class-consistent images. {#c-dn-scale}
Sweeping the guidance strength on class-conditional ImageNet, FID was minimized by a small amount of guidance, $w = 0.1$ or $0.3$ depending on the resolution, and the Inception score maximized by strong guidance, $w \ge 4$, with a clear trade-off in between; increasing the guidance strength decreased sample variety and increased the fidelity of individual samples. {#c-cfg-results}

## Limitations

Sampling is slow: a sample needs one network evaluation per step, run in sequence, where a GAN or a flow needs one pass.
Fewer steps with the same model lose quality, because the Gaussian reverse step is accurate only for small steps; `test_ddim_with_the_exact_eps_and_few_steps_shrinks_the_spread` shows the effect with an exact noise predictor, whose deterministic samples lose more than a fifth of the data's spread with 10 steps instead of 1000.
Likelihoods are an afterthought: the simple loss is not the bound, and DDPM's codelengths were not competitive, although later models trained on the bound with learned variances and continuous time reached competitive likelihoods.
There is no encoder in the DDPM sense: $\mathbf{x}_T$ from the stochastic sampler carries little information about the sample, and only the deterministic samplers give a latent code, one with the dimension of the data.
Guidance improves fidelity at the cost of diversity and, at high strength, saturated colors and repeated compositions.
Like any model of the training data, diffusion models can reproduce training images, more often for images duplicated in the data.

## Variants and descendants

**Score-based models** (NCSN, 2019) learn the score at a sequence of noise levels and sample with annealed Langevin dynamics; the SDE framework (2020) shows they and DDPM are discretizations of continuous processes, variance exploding and variance preserving.
**Improved DDPM** (2021) learns the reverse variances and uses a cosine schedule, which improves likelihoods and allows fewer steps.
**DDIM** and the probability flow ODE give deterministic samplers, and higher-order ODE solvers such as DPM-Solver and the samplers of EDM (2022) reach good samples in 10 to 50 steps.
**Distillation** trains a student to take two teacher steps in one, progressive distillation, or to map any point of a trajectory to its end, consistency models, for one-step to four-step sampling.
**Latent diffusion** (2022) runs the process in the latent space of an autoencoder and conditions on text with cross-attention; Stable Diffusion is one.
**Diffusion transformers** (2023) replace the U-Net by a transformer on patches of the latent.
**Flow matching and rectified flow** (2022) learn a velocity field along straight paths between noise and data; with Gaussian paths they are closely related to diffusion with a particular weighting and parameterization.
**Discrete diffusion** corrupts tokens by masking or by random replacement and is used for text and molecules.

## Notable models

Text-to-image systems are diffusion models: GLIDE (2021) used classifier-free guidance with text, DALL-E 2 (2022) decoded CLIP image embeddings with diffusion, Imagen (2022) conditioned a cascade of pixel diffusion models on a frozen T5 text encoder, and Stable Diffusion (2022) ran latent diffusion in public.
Video models such as Sora (2024) apply diffusion transformers to spacetime patches.
In audio, WaveGrad and DiffWave (2020) generated waveforms by diffusion.
In science, diffusion models generate protein backbones, as RFdiffusion does, and the structure module of AlphaFold 3 (2024) is a diffusion model over atom coordinates.
In robotics, diffusion policies generate action sequences.

## Implementations

The NumPy reference `impl/numpy/diffusion.py` implements the schedule, eq-forward-step and eq-forward-marginal, eq-posterior, the noise predictor of eq-eps-model with eq-time-embedding and a hand-written backward pass of eq-simple-loss, eq-reverse-mean, the ancestral step and sampler of eq-ddpm-step with $\sigma_t^2 = \beta_t$ or $\tilde\beta_t$, the DDIM step and sampler of eq-ddim-step and eq-ddim-sigma, the variational bound of eq-elbo with a Gaussian decoder of variance $\beta_1$, the score of eq-tweedie, the guidance of eq-guidance, Adam, a training loop, and the counts eq-params and eq-flops.
For data that are a mixture of isotropic Gaussians the exact noise prediction $\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol\epsilon \mid \mathbf{x}_t]$ has a closed form, `mixture_eps`, and the tests drive the samplers with it, which separates the error of the sampler from the error of the network.
The PyTorch version `impl/pytorch/diffusion.py` is an `EpsModel` module with the same parameters and autograd for the backward pass; `test_gradcheck` runs `torch.autograd.gradcheck` through it.
The JAX version `impl/jax/diffusion.py` is a set of pure functions of a parameter dictionary; `test_shared_fixture` takes the gradient with `jax.value_and_grad`, and `test_jitted_sampler_matches_a_python_loop` runs the deterministic sampler as a `jax.lax.fori_loop` under `jax.jit`.
All three load the shared fixture of `fixtures/make.py`, which fixes the parameters, the data, the steps $t \in \{1, 4, 7, 10\}$ and the noises, and checks the embedding, the noised points, the predictions, the loss and its gradients, the posterior, the reverse mean, an ancestral step and a DDIM step with $\eta = 0.5$ from $t$ to $\lfloor t/2 \rfloor$.

### Worked example

Take one dimension, $T = 2$ and $\beta = (0.1, 0.5)$, so $\alpha = (0.9, 0.5)$ and $\bar\alpha = (0.9, 0.45)$.
Noise $x_0 = 1$ to step 2 with $\epsilon = 0.5$: $x_2 = \sqrt{0.45} \cdot 1 + \sqrt{0.55} \cdot 0.5 = 0.6708 + 0.3708 = 1.0416$.
The posterior of eq-posterior has variance $\tilde\beta_2 = \tfrac{1 - 0.9}{1 - 0.45} \cdot 0.5 = 0.0909$, smaller than $\beta_2 = 0.5$ because $x_0$ is known, and mean $\tfrac{\sqrt{0.9} \cdot 0.5}{0.55} \cdot 1 + \tfrac{\sqrt{0.5} \cdot 0.1}{0.55} \cdot 1.0416 = 0.8624 + 0.1339 = 0.9964$.
A network that predicts the true noise $0.5$ gives by eq-reverse-mean $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{0.5}}\big(1.0416 - \tfrac{0.5}{\sqrt{0.55}} \cdot 0.5\big) = \tfrac{0.7045}{0.7071} = 0.9964$, the same, and the predicted data point $(1.0416 - \sqrt{0.55} \cdot 0.5) / \sqrt{0.45} = 1$ is exact.
With $\sigma_2^2 = \tilde\beta_2$ the weight of eq-eps-weight is $\tfrac{0.5^2}{2 \cdot 0.0909 \cdot 0.5 \cdot 0.55} = 5.0$; with $\sigma_2^2 = \beta_2$ it is $0.91$.
`test_worked_example` checks these numbers.

### Pitfalls

> [!pitfall]
> **Two meanings of alpha.** The DDIM paper writes $\alpha_t$ for what DDPM and this page call $\bar\alpha_t$, the cumulative product. Plugging DDPM's per-step $\alpha_t$ into the DDIM formulas gives a sampler that runs but produces noise. `test_ddim_with_eta_one_is_the_ddpm_step` checks that eq-ddim-step with $\eta = 1$ and $s = t - 1$ is the ancestral step with $\sigma_t^2 = \tilde\beta_t$, which holds only with the cumulative product.

> [!pitfall]
> **Expecting the loss to reach zero.** The noise is not a function of the noisy point, so the simple loss has a floor, the average conditional variance of the noise, which is large at small $t$. A loss that stops falling is not a bug; compare it with the floor. `test_learns_two_modes` checks that the loss ends within 0.05 of the exact floor of the two modes and starts more than 0.3 above it.

> [!pitfall]
> **Off-by-one in the steps.** Steps run from 1 to $T$, arrays usually from 0, and $\bar\alpha_0 = 1$ is needed for the last step of DDIM and for $\tilde\beta_1 = 0$. Indexing $\bar\alpha_{t-1}$ with the wrong offset shifts the whole schedule by one step. `test_posterior_is_bayes_rule` checks eq-posterior against Bayes' rule computed from eq-forward-step and eq-forward-marginal, and `test_forward_marginal_matches_the_chain` the marginal against the chain run step by step.

> [!pitfall]
> **Noise at the last step.** The ancestral sampler adds no noise when going to $\mathbf{x}_0$; with $\sigma_1^2 = \tilde\beta_1 = 0$ that is automatic, but with $\sigma_1^2 = \beta_1$ it has to be switched off. `test_ddpm_with_the_exact_eps_samples_the_data` propagates the exact moments through the sampler with both variances and lands within 1% of the data's spread at $T = 1000$.

> [!pitfall]
> **Few deterministic steps shrink the samples.** With $\eta = 0$ and few steps each step is an affine map that pulls points towards the mean, so samples are too concentrated even with a perfect noise predictor. `test_ddim_with_the_exact_eps_and_few_steps_shrinks_the_spread` checks that 1000 steps keep the spread within 1% and 10 steps lose more than a fifth of it.

> [!pitfall]
> **Reading the simple loss as a likelihood.** Eq-simple-loss is not the bound; it weights the steps differently, so a lower simple loss does not mean a better likelihood. Compute eq-elbo for likelihoods. `test_bound_is_above_the_models_negative_log_likelihood` checks that the bound lies above the model's exact negative log-likelihood, which lies above the entropy of the data, and `test_kl_term_is_a_weighted_eps_error` that each KL term is eq-eps-weight.

## History

Sohl-Dickstein, Weiss, Maheswaranathan and Ganguli posted the diffusion probabilistic model as arXiv 1503.03585 and published it at the 32nd International Conference on Machine Learning in 2015. {#c-history-sd}
The idea drew on annealed importance sampling, Langevin dynamics and the reversal of diffusions in physics, and on denoising score matching, which Vincent had related to denoising autoencoders in 2011.
The approach saw little follow-up for four years.
Song and Ermon's noise conditional score networks (2019) made score-based generation work on images, and Ho, Jain and Abbeel published denoising diffusion probabilistic models at the 34th Conference on Neural Information Processing Systems, NeurIPS 2020. {#c-history-ddpm}
DDPM's samples matched GANs on CIFAR-10, and within a year DDIM, the SDE framework, improved DDPM and Dhariwal and Nichol's guided models had made diffusion faster, better understood and better than GANs on ImageNet.
Classifier-free guidance, first presented at a NeurIPS 2021 workshop, and latent diffusion opened text-to-image generation to the public in 2022, and diffusion transformers carried the approach to video.
